# Ensembles, not architectures, win CAFA

The clearest finding across CAFA-5's post-mortems and the one detailed CAFA-6 write-up available today is that **no single architecture — not a protein language model (PLM), not a structure-based graph network, not an ontology-aware loss — wins this competition on its own**; every top-placing solution examined is a stacked ensemble of heterogeneous evidence (PLM embeddings, sequence alignment, literature text mining, taxonomy, electronic annotations), fused through learning-to-rank, gradient boosting, or graph convolutional stacking. CAFA-6 (launched October 2025, entry deadline late January 2026, $50,000 prize pool from Iowa State, Northeastern, UniProt, and ISCB) preserves CAFA-5's core structure almost unchanged: three independently-scored Gene Ontology aspects (molecular function, biological process, cellular component), an information-accretion-weighted Fmax as the leaderboard metric, and — critically — a evaluation regime now dominated by "partial knowledge" (PK) proteins that already carry some annotations and need new ones predicted, a setting CAFA-5 found methods perform **47–60% worse on** than on totally novel proteins ([CAFA5 report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13142333/)). PLM embeddings (ESM-2, ProtT5, ESM-1b) have become the near-universal base feature layer, but the field's own benchmarks show they rarely beat sequence-similarity search or ontology-aware losses alone, and the biggest, most durable technique is a Fudan-lab ensembling lineage (GOLabeler → NetGO → GOCurator) that has placed at or near the top of CAFA3, CAFA4, and CAFA5 by successively bolting new evidence sources onto the same stacking framework. Meanwhile the field's most honest, well-documented weakness — that GO annotations are an open-world, systematically biased sample of true protein function, not a closed label set — is repeatedly acknowledged by CAFA's own organizers but almost never addressed with formal correction techniques (positive-unlabeled learning remains a niche, one-paper intersection), leaving the incompleteness problem handled mostly at the evaluation-design level (time-delayed benchmarks) rather than the training-label level.

## CAFA-6 keeps CAFA-5's evaluation regime, and "partial knowledge" now dominates the score

CAFA (Critical Assessment of Function Annotation) is a triennial, CASP-style blind-prediction challenge run by an academic consortium centered on Iowa State University and Northeastern University, in which participants predict which Gene Ontology terms apply to proteins whose true functions are locked in at a submission deadline (t0) and only resolved later (te) from newly accumulated experimental evidence ([CAFA5 report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13142333/)). CAFA-5 (2023–2025) was the first round hosted on Kaggle, drawing **1,625 teams and 2,850 submitted models** — a 22-fold jump in participation over CAFA-4 — by compressing the field's full metric suite into a single leaderboard number: **information-accretion (IA)-weighted Fmax**, averaged across the three GO aspects and computed only on the "No-Knowledge" (NK) and "Limited-Knowledge" (LK) settings live, with the newer "Partial-Knowledge" (PK) category held back for post-hoc analysis ([CAFA5 report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13142333/)). IA weighting is the mechanism that keeps the metric honest under GO's long-tailed term frequency: a term's information content is defined as −log₂ Pr(term | its parents are already annotated), so predicting a rare, deep, specific term scores far more than predicting a common, shallow one, which directly discourages majority-class-style baselines from scoring well despite high raw accuracy ([CAFA5 report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13142333/)). CAFA-6 launched in October 2025 with the same $50,000-prize, Iowa-State/Northeastern/UniProt/ISCB structure ([Kaggle launch announcement](https://x.com/kaggle/status/1978878346130784672)), and a genuine, independently reproducible CAFA-6 solution write-up (11th place) confirms the same evaluation skeleton persisted: its author reports scoring against `mean f_micro_w` across "NK, LK, PK" settings ([GitHub: T0chka/cafa6_11th_place_solution](https://github.com/T0chka/cafa6_11th_place_solution)) — though the metric's exact name changed from CAFA-5's "modified F1-score," a detail anyone porting a CAFA-5-tuned threshold or post-processing heuristic into CAFA-6 should not assume is cosmetic.

The single biggest structural shift a technique-comparison has to account for is that PK is no longer a niche edge case. In CAFA-5's final evaluation benchmark, PK accounted for **21,415 of roughly 25,000 evaluated proteins and 163,349 of roughly 234,000 accumulated annotations — 69.7% of the total scoring mass** ([CAFA5 report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13142333/)), and performance on it is dramatically worse than on NK/LK: CAFA-5 assessors measured **55.6%, 47.8%, and 59.7% drops in Fmax_μ** for biological process, cellular component, and molecular function respectively, comparing NK against PK, attributing this partly to PK terms sitting deeper in the ontology (intrinsically harder) and partly to the plain fact that "model developers may not have optimized for this new setting" ([CAFA5 report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13142333/)). That second clause matters: it means the PK gap is at least partially an artifact of the field still building "sequence → GO term" cold-start classifiers for a task that has quietly become majority-PK, i.e. closer to link prediction on a partially-observed annotation graph than to zero-shot sequence classification. Any architecture comparison that only reports NK/LK-style performance is now describing a minority of the real scoring surface.

## Protein language model embeddings are table stakes, not the winning move

By roughly 2022–2024 the field converged on a common recipe: extract per-residue or mean-pooled embeddings from a large pretrained PLM (ESM-1b, ESM-2, ProtT5/ProtTrans) and feed them into a comparatively small downstream head — logistic regression, an MLP, an attention-pooling layer, or a triplet network — displacing hand-engineered k-mer, BLAST-hit, and InterPro-domain features as the dominant single input stream ([SPROF-GO](https://academic.oup.com/bib/article/24/3/bbad117/7085635); [NetGO 3.0](https://academic.oup.com/gpb/article/21/2/349/7585485); [ATGO](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1010793)). The CAFA-5 organizers' own assessment credits this shift explicitly: "the strong performance of the protein language model (pLM) embedding baseline shows how the development of protein models outside the function prediction space can help support the development of function predictors" ([CAFA5 report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13142333/)). SPROF-GO — ProtTrans embeddings through dual MLPs combined via self-attention pooling, plus a homology-based label-diffusion post-processing step — self-reports beating prior sequence-based and even network-based methods by **14.5%, 27.3%, and 10.1% AUPR** across the three GO aspects ([SPROF-GO](https://academic.oup.com/bib/article/24/3/bbad117/7085635)). TALE goes further, jointly learning a label embedding for GO terms alongside the sequence embedding so that term-scoring becomes a similarity computation rather than a fixed classifier head, specifically targeting generalization to rare labels and low-homology proteins ([TALE](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/37/18/2825/6182677)).

But the learning that matters most for a technique comparison is what PLM embeddings *don't* buy on their own. Every CAFA-5 top-placing system examined treats PLM embeddings as one input stream inside a larger pipeline, not as the whole model: GOCurator (1st place, self-reported) tested ESM-1b, ESM-2, and ProtT5 and found "little difference in the scores achieved by these methods" ([GOCurator writeup](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/cafa-5-protein-function-prediction/writeups/gocurator-1st-place-solution-for-the-cafa5)) — implying PLM *choice* barely matters once embeddings are just one ingredient among several. StarFunc (5th place) found that a single sequence-based deep-learning component (built on the PLM-based SPROF-GO) alone captured **98.7% of its ensemble's performance** ([5th place writeup](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/cafa-5-protein-function-prediction/writeups/hfm7zc-5th-place-solution-for-the-cafa-5-protein-f)), while team Evans (6th place) found their PLM-only network already achieved "approximately 90–95%" of their full blended model's performance ([6th place writeup](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/cafa-5-protein-function-prediction/writeups/evans-6th-place-solution-for-the-cafa-5-protein-fu)). Read together, these numbers say PLM embeddings deliver most of the achievable signal cheaply, but the remaining single-digit-to-low-double-digit percentage points — which decide competition placement — come from elsewhere. Two independent top-6 CAFA-5 teams (Evans and the Freddolino Lab's Top6 entry) also reported that standard binary cross-entropy loss is a genuine weak point at this label cardinality and imbalance, switching instead to IA-weighted soft-F1-style losses or ranking losses ([6th place writeup](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/cafa-5-protein-function-prediction/writeups/evans-6th-place-solution-for-the-cafa-5-protein-fu); [ProtBoost paper survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04529)). On the cost side, the largest PLMs carry real practical limits: ESM-2 15B "cannot be fine-tuned even with high-memory GPUs such as the NVidia H100" without parameter-efficient methods like LoRA ([PLM efficiency review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12481099/)), and a transfer-learning benchmark found medium-sized models like ESM-2 650M "fell only slightly behind" ESM-2 15B on downstream transfer tasks "despite being many times smaller" ([Scientific Reports, medium PLMs](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-05674-x)) — evidence that scaling PLM size specifically for GO prediction hits diminishing returns well before the largest available checkpoints.

## Structure and ontology awareness pay off mostly at the long tail of rare GO terms

Two further technique families try to squeeze out the remaining signal PLM embeddings leave on the table: incorporating 3D structure, and exploiting the GO DAG's own logical structure (the "true path rule" — a protein annotated with a term is implicitly annotated with all its ancestors). Structure-based methods build graph representations from residue contact or distance information and process them with graph convolutional or geometric-vector-perceptron networks — DeepFRI fuses an LSTM sequence encoder with a contact-map GCN ([DeepFRI](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-23303-9)), while PANDA-3D combines AlphaFold-predicted structure via GVP-GNN layers with ESM sequence embeddings and transformer decoder layers ([PANDA-3D](https://academic.oup.com/nargab/article/6/3/lqae094/7728018)). The empirical case for structure is genuinely mixed rather than a clean win: an independent 14-method benchmark found structure/PPI-integrating DeepGraphGO reached Fmax 0.427 on biological process, but the pure-sequence, ontology-aware DeepGOZero+ reached **0.463 — beating it — while requiring no predicted structure at all**, and the same benchmark characterizes DeepFRI's and GAT-GO's structural gains as only "modest" ([comprehensive benchmark](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10883809/)). Even PANDA-3D's own permutation feature-importance analysis ranks sequence-derived ESM features among its top three most important inputs, undercutting any claim that structure is independently dominant even in the strongest structure-based system found ([PANDA-3D](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11302463/)). Given that structural methods also carry a real cost — dependence on real or AlphaFold-predicted structures and GCN/GVP training — the cost-benefit case for adding structure on top of a well-designed sequence pipeline is weaker than early "structure helps" narratives suggested, and likely reflects comparisons against weak sequence-only baselines more than a robust structural advantage.

Ontology-hierarchy awareness has a more clearly delineated payoff: it concentrates specifically at the long tail of rare, poorly-annotated GO terms — exactly the terms IA-weighting rewards most. DeepGOZero jointly optimizes standard binary cross-entropy with four "normal form" losses derived from EL Embeddings that encode GO's Description Logic axioms directly as geometric constraints, enabling **zero-shot prediction for terms with fewer than 100 experimentally annotated proteins** by transferring information through the ~12,000 formal definition axioms GO carries ([DeepGOZero](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/38/Supplement_1/i238/6617515)). In the same 14-method benchmark, ontology-aware DeepGOZero+ scored 0.618 AUPR on molecular function versus flat DeepGOCNN's 0.302 — roughly double, though this compares across systems that also differ in encoder and augmentation, so the magnitude is suggestive rather than a clean isolated ablation ([comprehensive benchmark](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10883809/)). TALE takes a related but distinct route, using a constrained loss that forces ancestor terms to score at least as high as their descendants, which the same benchmark credits with "superior performance on depth-weighted metrics" ([TALE](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/37/18/2825/6182677)). A third route — ProTranslator, which uses natural-language GO-term descriptions rather than formal axioms — reports its largest gains over DeepGOPlus precisely on terms with 1–20 annotated proteins, "with improvement larger for terms that have fewer annotated proteins" ([ProTranslator](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.10286)). That three independently-developed techniques (formal-logic embeddings, hierarchy-constrained losses, and text-based zero-shot transfer) all converge on solving the same rare-term problem, via genuinely different knowledge sources, is itself informative: it suggests the rare-term gap is tractable through multiple independent signal channels beyond raw training examples, and a system combining more than one of them — which no source in this research reviewed directly — is a plausible, currently untested opportunity.

## Stacked ensembles of heterogeneous evidence are the actual winning recipe

The most durable, cross-generational pattern in CAFA is not a model family at all — it is a combination strategy. GOLabeler (CAFA3's top performer) fused five component classifiers spanning term-frequency priors, BLAST-KNN, sequence k-mers, InterPro domains, and biophysical properties via a learning-to-rank framework ([GOLabeler](https://scite.ai/reports/golabeler-improving-sequence-based-large-scale-protein-68jx4gM)); the same Fudan University lab (led by Shanfeng Zhu) evolved this into NetGO, then NetGO 3.0 (which simply swapped in an ESM-1b-based component for an older RNN one while keeping the ensemble architecture intact) ([NetGO 3.0](https://academic.oup.com/gpb/article/21/2/349/7585485)), and finally into GOCurator, the self-reported CAFA-5 first-place solution, which layered in literature text mining, protein structural information, and a two-stage literature-to-GO-term matcher called GORetriever on top of the same lineage ([GOCurator writeup](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/cafa-5-protein-function-prediction/writeups/gocurator-1st-place-solution-for-the-cafa5)). This is a genuinely rare case of one research group placing at or near the top across three consecutive CAFA rounds (CAFA3, CAFA4, CAFA5) by successively bolting new evidence types onto an unchanged combination framework, rather than by replacing the ensembling architecture — evidence that the stacking layer itself, more than any specific underlying model, is where durable competitive advantage lives in this benchmark.

ProtBoost (self-reported CAFA-5 2nd place, private-leaderboard score 0.5824) makes the mechanism most visible through its own staged ablation table: starting from a Ridge-regression baseline on Prot-T5 embeddings alone (≈0.47), adding a novel gradient-boosting library the team built specifically for thousands of simultaneous multi-label targets (Py-Boost, ≈0.51), one-hot taxon features (≈0.53), conditional-probability re-parameterization of GO's hierarchy (≈0.55), external GOA electronic annotations (≈0.57), neural-network ensembling (≈0.59), and finally a graph convolutional network that stacks all prior models together with the GO graph structure (≈0.62) — each stage adding a measurable, non-trivial increment ([ProtBoost paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04529)). Critically, reported ensembling *lift* varies enormously by team: it depends on how genuinely uncorrelated the component predictors are. StarFunc's 98.7%-from-one-component finding and team Evans's 90–95%-from-PLM-alone finding (cited above) show ensembling barely moving the needle when components are redundant, whereas ProtBoost's staged gains and PROTGOAT's five-PLM-plus-literature fusion (self-reported 4th place) show substantial lift when components genuinely diverge — sequence vs. structure vs. literature vs. alignment each catch different failure modes ([PROTGOAT](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.04.01.587572v1.full)). The pattern reproduces directly into CAFA-6: the one independently verified CAFA-6 write-up (11th place) combines six components — a hierarchy-aware MLP on ESM2-3B embeddings, an MLP-with-residual-blocks on concatenated ProtT5+ESM1b embeddings, Py-Boost gradient boosting (the same library ProtBoost introduced two years earlier), a BLAST-KNN retrieval baseline, a naive frequency prior, and non-experimental UniProt annotations — combined via an XGBoost learning-to-rank meta-model, then post-processed by propagating predictions through the GO hierarchy and truncating to the top 500 terms per protein ([GitHub: T0chka/cafa6_11th_place_solution](https://github.com/T0chka/cafa6_11th_place_solution)). Py-Boost's unprompted reappearance in an independent team's CAFA-6 toolkit is a strong signal it has become a genuine community-adopted tool for this problem shape, not a one-off trick. The top CAFA-6 placements themselves (1st through 5th) could not be independently verified in this research — one lead naming a 1st-place solution by user "chya" and a 2nd-place solution described as using "py-boost, GCN, and articles data" could not be corroborated by direct search and is suspiciously identical to CAFA-5's ProtBoost description, raising a real possibility of AI-summarization cross-contamination rather than genuine CAFA-6 content; this should be treated as an open item, not a confirmed fact.

## The open-world annotation problem is acknowledged everywhere but corrected almost nowhere

Underneath every architecture choice sits a labeling problem CAFA's own organizers state bluntly: "absence of evidence of function does not imply absence of function," a stance the GO Consortium's own handbook calls the Open World Assumption ([Gaudet & Dessimoz, GO Handbook](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.01875)). This is not a benign, random missingness — it is systematically biased toward well-studied proteins. A retrospective re-analysis of CAFA1 found that **14.7% of predictions scored as false positives were later confirmed correct within five years** as more literature was curated ([Dessimoz, Škunca & Thomas, Trends in Genetics 2013](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1411455/2/Dessimoz2013-CAFA%20and%20the%20Open%20World%20of%20protein%20function%20predictions.pdf)), and a separate quantitative study found the Gini coefficient of GO annotation distribution across genes rose from **0.25 in 2001 to 0.47 in 2017**, with gene-level publication count correlating strongly with annotation count (Spearman's ρ = 0.110, p = 2.1×10⁻¹⁶) but not with independent molecular evidence of biological importance (ρ = −0.003, p = 0.836) — annotation density tracks how much a gene has been studied, not how functionally important it is ([Haynes et al., Scientific Reports 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-19333-x)). This "rich-getting-richer" bias directly undermines the Selected-Completely-At-Random (SCAR) assumption that classical positive-unlabeled (PU) learning theory requires: Elkan-Noto's label-frequency rescaling, kernel mean embedding (KM2), and TIcE's decision-tree-based class-prior estimator all assume each true positive is independently labeled with a fixed probability, which is precisely false for GO ([Elkan & Noto, KDD 2008](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~elkan/posonly.pdf); [Ramaswamy, Scott & Tewari, ICML 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.02501); [Bekker & Davis, ECML-PKDD 2018](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v94/bekker18a/bekker18a.pdf)). None of these estimators is designed to detect or correct SAR-type bias — they can silently return a confidently wrong prior if their SCAR assumption is violated, since nothing in their formulation checks it.

Despite the problem being well-documented, the field's actual response is concentrated almost entirely on evaluation-methodology design rather than training-time label correction: CAFA's own NK/LK/PK time-delayed benchmark structure, evidence-code filtering (training only on experimentally-validated codes, excluding electronic annotations), and dedicated curated-negative resources like the NoGO database all cope with open-world incompleteness by carefully defining *what gets scored*, not by fixing what counts as a negative label during training ([Fernández & Friedberg, NoGO database](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1003644)). The one purpose-built modern PU-learning method for GO prediction, PU-GO (2024), frames multi-label GO annotation as a PU-ranking problem with a GO-structure-aware class prior, and reports outperforming DeepGO-CNN, DeepGOZero, and a plain MLP(ESM2) baseline on Fmax/Smin/AUPR across all three aspects, with PU-GO+Diamond achieving the best class-centric AUC among all methods it compared against ([PU-GO, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11211813/)). But PU-GO's own limitations — a prior built from raw annotation frequency alone, no use of curated negatives, and evaluation with only one architecture — combined with the near-total absence of a second modern, deep-learning-era paper attempting the same PU-plus-GO combination, indicates this intersection remains a genuinely underexplored corner of the field rather than a mature technique family, despite the open-world problem being one of CAFA's own most repeatedly stated concerns.

## Conclusion

The field's understanding of its own bottleneck has shifted meaningfully since CAFA-5: the historical headline task — predicting function for a totally novel protein — is empirically no longer the hardest or even the most common real case. Predicting *new* annotations for proteins that already carry some GO terms (PK) now accounts for 70% of scoring mass and suffers a 48–60% Fmax drop relative to novel-protein prediction, and CAFA-5's own assessors attribute part of that gap simply to the field not yet building methods for it ([CAFA5 report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13142333/)). That reframes the practical question for anyone building a CAFA-6 pipeline today: architecture choice among PLM backbones is close to a solved, low-variance decision, and the marginal points that separate leaderboard placements come from (a) how many genuinely uncorrelated evidence sources feed into the ensemble, (b) whether the pipeline exploits GO's ontology structure for the long tail of rare terms rather than treating labels as flat, and — the most conspicuous gap this research surfaced — (c) whether the training signal itself treats "unannotated" as a confident negative or as the systematically-biased, unresolved open-world label it actually is. The near-absence of a mature PU-learning literature for GO prediction, set against how thoroughly CAFA's own organizers document the bias, suggests this is the one technique family where a genuinely novel contribution (rather than another ensemble layer or another PLM swap) is still available to whoever tackles it carefully enough to survive GO's long-tailed, non-SCAR annotation distribution.
