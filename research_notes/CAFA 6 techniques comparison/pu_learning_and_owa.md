# The Open World Assumption in GO Annotation / Protein Function Prediction, and PU-Learning Approaches to It

## How significant is annotation bias in GO — is there published evidence that better-studied proteins gain more true annotations over time, implying systematic incompleteness rather than random missingness?

### Takeaway
Yes — this is well documented and quantified from multiple independent angles: a direct retrospective CAFA re-analysis found ~14.7% of "false positive" predictions were later confirmed true within 5 years, annotation inequality (Gini coefficient) across genes has been rising for two decades, and annotation counts track publication/study intensity far more than they track underlying molecular evidence. This confirms the incompleteness is annotation-bias-driven (Selected-At-Random / non-random missingness), not benign random missingness.

### Cited Findings
- Dessimoz, Škunca & Thomas simulated the CAFA1 evaluation retrospectively using successive UniProt-GOA releases (2007–2013): predictions to CLC4E_MOUSE that were counted as "false positives" under CAFA's closed-world scoring were later confirmed correct by curators once more literature had been reviewed — motivating the general test. Across the full CAFA1 benchmark, "~14.7% of the predictions initially deemed as false positives were later confirmed to be correct" when checked against database releases up to 5 years later, and the proportion of eventually-confirmed spurious false positives rose monotonically with the length of the follow-up window (from ~10% within 2 years to ~14.7% within 5 years) — [Dessimoz, Škunca & Thomas, "CAFA and the Open World of protein function predictions," Trends in Genetics 2013](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1411455/2/Dessimoz2013-CAFA%20and%20the%20Open%20World%20of%20protein%20function%20predictions.pdf)
- The same paper reports that as of its writing only ~2,500 of ~530,000 (0.48%) experimentally-confirmed molecular-function/biological-process annotations in UniProt-GOA were explicit negative ("NOT") annotations — i.e. the database almost never records that a protein lacks a function, so an unannotated pair is essentially uninterpretable as a true negative — [Dessimoz et al. 2013](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1411455/2/Dessimoz2013-CAFA%20and%20the%20Open%20World%20of%20protein%20function%20predictions.pdf)
- The GO Consortium's own handbook chapter states plainly that "the information contained in the GO database ... is necessarily incomplete. Thus, absence of evidence of function does not imply absence of function. This is referred to as the Open World Assumption," and separately notes that "the incompleteness in the GO is very uneven" across the ontology and across genes/organisms — [Gaudet & Dessimoz, "Gene Ontology: Pitfalls, Biases, Remedies" (GO Handbook)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.01875)
- The GO handbook cites Schnoes et al.'s finding that annotations derived from high-throughput experiments — which tend to cover a limited, high-level set of functions and thus articially depress information content — make up as much as 25% of annotations in the GO database, another source of systematic (non-random) bias in what gets annotated — [Gaudet & Dessimoz, GO Handbook](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.01875)
- A quantitative bias study found the Gini coefficient of GO annotation distribution across genes rose from 0.25 in 2001 to 0.47 in 2017, indicating growing (not shrinking) inequality in which genes accumulate annotations, and that "the actual changes in inequality most closely matched the models of increasingly biased ['rich-getting-richer'] growth" rather than models of even/random annotation spread — [Haynes et al., "Gene annotation bias impedes biomedical research," Scientific Reports 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-19333-x)
- The same study found publication count for a gene correlates strongly with its GO annotation count (Spearman's ρ = 0.110, p = 2.1×10⁻¹⁶) but does *not* correlate with independent molecular evidence of disease relevance (ρ = −0.003, p = 0.836; only 19.5% of published disease-gene associations were corroborated by gene-expression analyses at 5% FDR across 104 diseases/41,000+ patients) — i.e. GO annotation density tracks how much a gene has been studied, not how biologically important/functionally rich it truly is — [Haynes et al. 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-19333-x)
- PU-GO's motivating analysis (see next question) independently quantifies the same phenomenon at the database-release level: 2,236 proteins gained 4,236 brand-new experimental annotations between two UniProtKB versions, plus 31,149 additional annotations became inferable only via true-path-rule propagation from those new assertions — concrete evidence that a fixed set of already-known proteins keeps gaining true annotations that existed all along but weren't yet recorded — [Boudin et al., "Predicting protein functions using positive-unlabeled ranking with ontology-based priors," Bioinformatics/ISMB 2024, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11211813/)

### Inferences
- Together, these sources establish that GO incompleteness is not "missing at random" (MAR/MCAR) — it is a Selected-At-Random (SAR)-type bias correlated with study intensity, publication volume, and evidence-generation technology (high-throughput vs. targeted). This directly undermines the SCAR (Selected Completely At Random) assumption that most classical PU-learning class-prior estimators require (see below), because whether a true positive gets labeled is not independent of the protein/GO-term pair's covariates.
- The Dessimoz et al. 5-year-window finding (10% → 14.7% error rate in "false positives" as more time passes) also implies the openness problem gets *worse*, not better, the longer the evaluation horizon — an important caveat for any offline validation set built from "the current GO release minus withheld terms," since even a multi-year-old snapshot likely still undercounts true annotations for its benchmark proteins.

### Gaps
- No source found gives a direct, controlled comparison of annotation completeness for "well-studied" vs. "poorly-studied" organisms (as opposed to well-studied vs. poorly-studied genes within a single organism/dataset) with a quantitative completeness metric; the Haynes et al. Gini-coefficient analysis is gene-level, primarily within human/model-organism data, not an explicit organism-vs-organism comparison.

## What positive-unlabeled (PU) learning techniques have been specifically applied to GO term / protein function prediction (as opposed to generic PU-learning papers)?

### Takeaway
The intersection of PU-learning and GO-term-level protein function prediction is real but sparse: one recent, purpose-built method (PU-GO, 2024) explicitly frames multi-label GO prediction as a PU-ranking problem with ontology-derived priors, and a handful of much older (2008–2010) yeast-gene-function methods used ad hoc "reliable negative selection" PU strategies; PU-learning applications named in a broader bioinformatics review otherwise concentrate on adjacent-but-different tasks (site/variant prediction) rather than full GO multi-label annotation.

### Cited Findings
- **PU-GO** (Boudin et al., ISMB/Bioinformatics 2024) treats GO-term prediction as multi-label PU learning: it uses an empirical-risk-minimization formulation with a non-negative-style risk correction (explicit "margin factor β to prevent negative risk values," in the spirit of nnPU) combined with a **ranking loss** — rather than pushing unlabeled-sample scores toward 0, it only requires positive samples to score higher than unlabeled ones, which the authors found less destructive than a hard positive/negative split when many "unlabeled" pairs are actually true — [PMC11211813](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11211813/); [bioRxiv preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.01.28.577662.full.pdf); [Oxford Academic version](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/40/Supplement_1/i401/7700893)
- PU-GO's class-prior construction is GO-structure-aware rather than a single global constant: prior for term *i* is πᵢ = π₀ · Sᵢ/Sₘₐₓ, where Sᵢ is the term's normalized annotation frequency (respecting the true-path rule, so terms near the ontology root — which accumulate more annotations by propagation — get higher priors) and π₀ is a single tunable hyperparameter shared across all terms — [PMC11211813](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11211813/)
- On both similarity-based and CAFA-style time-delayed benchmarks across MFO/BPO/CCO, PU-GO outperformed DeepGO-CNN, DeepGOZero, and a plain MLP(ESM2) baseline on Fmax/Smin/AUPR/AUC; combined with Diamond sequence-similarity search, PU-GO+Diamond achieved the best class-centric AUC of all methods compared (including ATGO+, NetGO3, SPROF-GO), with competitive but not always best Fmax/Smin — [PMC11211813](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11211813/)
- PU-GO's own stated limitations: its prior uses only raw annotation-frequency information (no semantic similarity between GO terms), it makes no use of experimentally curated negative annotations, it requires empirical tuning of the π₀ hyperparameter, and it was evaluated only with one architecture (ESM2 embeddings + MLP) — [PMC11211813](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11211813/)
- A 2022 review of PU-learning in bioinformatics identifies two much older gene-function-prediction methods (referred to as "AGPS" and "SPE/RNE," 2008–2010) that applied PU learning to **yeast gene function prediction** using GO annotations as the positive-only label source, via "negative expansion" (iteratively identifying likely-negative examples from the unlabeled pool) combined with SVM classifiers — because "GO only provide[s] GO annotations for proteins/genes (i.e. positive samples), while the negative annotation ... are usually not available" — [Zhang et al., "Positive-unlabeled learning in bioinformatics and computational biology: a brief review," Briefings in Bioinformatics 2022](https://academic.oup.com/bib/article/23/1/bbab461/6415313)
- The same review names AlphaMax-based class-prior estimation as used in **MutPred2** and **GlycoMine_PU** — but these are variant-effect and glycosylation-site prediction tools, not GO-term multi-label annotation prediction; they are adjacent PU-in-bioinformatics applications, not examples of PU applied to the GO-annotation task itself — [Zhang et al. 2022](https://academic.oup.com/bib/article/23/1/bbab461/6415313)
- An older paper, "Predicting gene function using few positive examples and unlabeled ones" (BMC Genomics 2010), is explicitly framed around GO-term prediction from very few positive examples using PU-style learning; full text was not retrieved in this research pass, so only the title/framing is confirmed, not its specific technique or reported effectiveness — [BMC Genomics 2010, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1471-2164-11-S2-S11)

### Inferences
- PU-GO appears to be the first (or among the very first) methods to combine a formal PU-learning risk correction *and* a GO-hierarchy-informed prior *and* evaluation against modern CAFA-style benchmarks and modern baselines (DeepGO family, PLM-embedding methods) — the 2008–2010 methods predate the deep-learning/PLM era of protein function prediction entirely and used much simpler features/classifiers.
- The scarcity of directly-on-topic papers (essentially one modern method plus a couple of pre-deep-learning precedents) suggests PU-learning has not been broadly adopted as a mainstream technique in the CAFA-style protein function prediction community, despite the OWA problem being explicitly and repeatedly acknowledged in that same community's own methodology papers (see CAFA/Dessimoz findings above).

### Gaps
- As anticipated in the task framing, this does appear to be a genuinely underexplored intersection: no second modern (post-2020, deep-learning-based) paper combining explicit PU-learning theory with full multi-label GO-term prediction was found beyond PU-GO. It's possible such work exists but wasn't surfaced by the search terms used here (a deeper literature search, e.g. via Google Scholar citation-chasing from PU-GO's own references, would be needed to rule this out definitively).
- Could not retrieve full methodological detail on the BMC Genomics 2010 "few positive examples" paper or the "AGPS"/"SPE_RNE" methods beyond what the 2022 review paraphrased; their actual reported effectiveness (metrics, comparison baselines) is unconfirmed here.

## What are the classical PU-learning class-prior estimation techniques (Elkan-Noto, KM2, TIcE, nnPU) — core idea, data conditions needed, and known failure modes?

### Takeaway
All four techniques were designed under the SCAR (Selected Completely At Random) assumption and require a non-trivial number of confidently-labeled positive examples to produce a stable class-prior/label-frequency estimate; they differ mainly in computational cost and robustness, but none of them is designed to correct for SAR-type bias (annotation driven by how well-studied a gene is), which is exactly the bias documented in GO. Sample-size/robustness is a well-established weak point for all of them, which matters directly for GO's long-tailed term-frequency distribution (see next question).

### Cited Findings
- **Elkan & Noto (2008)**: under the SCAR assumption (each true positive is independently labeled with fixed probability c = P(s=1|y=1), the "label frequency"), a probabilistic classifier g(x) trained to distinguish labeled vs. unlabeled examples satisfies f(x) = g(x)/c, so the true positive-class posterior can be recovered from g(x) by rescaling with an estimate of c. Elkan's recommended estimator (e1) averages g(x) over a held-out set of known-positive validation examples. In their original experiments a validation set of as few as ~20 labeled positive examples produced a c-estimate close to the true value in at least one case — suggesting the method *can* work with modest labeled-positive counts, though this was reported as a specific empirical result, not a general guarantee — [search-aggregated summary citing Elkan & Noto, "Learning classifiers from only positive and unlabeled data," KDD 2008](https://cseweb.ucsd.edu/~elkan/posonly.pdf)
- **KM2 / kernel mean embedding (Ramaswamy, Scott & Tewari, 2016)**: frames class-prior (mixture proportion) estimation as recovering a mixture weight via kernel mean embeddings of the positive and unlabeled distributions in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, reducing the estimation problem to a sequence of quadratic programs; the paper provides the first efficient MPE algorithm with proven convergence rates. It was benchmarked on six moderate-sized UCI datasets (waveform, mushroom, pageblocks, shuttle, spambase, digits) where it performed comparably to or better than AlphaMax, Elkan-Noto (EN), and ROC-based estimators on 5 of 6 datasets — [Ramaswamy, Scott & Tewari, "Mixture Proportion Estimation via Kernel Embeddings of Distributions," ICML 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.02501)
- **TIcE (Bekker & Davis, 2018)**: estimates the SCAR label frequency c by decision-tree induction — searching for subsets ("leaves") of the unlabeled+labeled data where the ratio of labeled to total examples is maximized; in a subset that is "pure" (all true positives), that ratio equals c exactly, and in general it is a valid *lower bound* on c under SCAR. TIcE was reported to be roughly an order of magnitude faster than comparable methods (≈0.76 ms/example vs. 21.96 ms for EN, 38.47 ms for AlphaMax, 47.28 ms for KM1/KM2) — [Bekker & Davis, "Estimating the Class Prior in Positive and Unlabeled Data Through Decision Tree Induction," LIDTA/ECML-PKDD 2018](http://proceedings.mlr.press/v94/bekker18a/bekker18a.pdf); efficiency figures also reported via a comparative summary in [arxiv.org/html/2303.08269 (PULSNAR paper)](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.08269)
- TIcE's bound is explicitly stated to depend on SCAR holding, and its authors/reviewers note SCAR "is unrealistic in many instances of PU learning" with real labeling bias (electronic health records, recommendation systems are the cited examples), with follow-up work needed to extend the approach to non-SCAR ("SNAR"/SAR) settings — [PULSNAR paper summary, arXiv 2303.08269](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.08269); Bekker & Davis's own follow-up work explicitly addresses moving "Beyond the Selected Completely At Random Assumption" — [Bekker et al., ECML 2019](https://jessa.github.io/assets/pdf/bekker2019ecml.pdf)
- **nnPU (du Plessis, Niu & Sugiyama; formalized with the non-negative correction by Kiryo, Niu, du Plessis & Sugiyama, NeurIPS 2017)**: the earlier *unbiased* PU risk estimator (uPU) can produce a negative empirical risk when paired with flexible models (e.g. deep nets) and limited positive data, which drives severe overfitting during training. nnPU fixes this by wrapping the negative-risk-prone term in a max(0, ·) rectifier, flooring the risk contribution at zero once it would otherwise go negative; this was shown empirically to remove the overfitting behavior observed with the unbiased estimator — [Kiryo et al., "Positive-Unlabeled Learning with Non-Negative Risk Estimator," NeurIPS 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.00593)
- nnPU still requires the class prior π = P(y=1) as an external input to its loss function — it does not estimate the prior itself, so it is typically paired with one of the class-prior estimators above (e.g. KM2, TIcE, Elkan-Noto), and inherits whatever bias or variance that upstream prior estimate carries — [Kiryo et al. 2017](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1703.00593)
- The 2022 bioinformatics PU-learning review separately flags "overfitting risk ... when the unlabeled training set is overly small" and sensitivity to imbalance between the sizes of the positive and unlabeled sets as general failure modes across PU methods applied in bioinformatics, recommending sub-sampling to rebalance set sizes as a practical mitigation — [Zhang et al. 2022](https://academic.oup.com/bib/article/23/1/bbab461/6415313)

### Inferences
- All four techniques converge on the same structural requirement: a class-prior/label-frequency estimate that is only as reliable as (a) SCAR holding and (b) there being "enough" labeled positive examples to make the estimator's variance small. None of EN, KM2, or TIcE was designed to detect or correct SAR-type bias directly — they can silently return a confidently-wrong prior estimate if SCAR is violated, since nothing in their formulation checks the assumption.
- Because nnPU consumes a class prior as an input rather than deriving one internally, any of its documented benefits (removing uPU's negative-risk overfitting) only address model-training instability *given* a correct prior — they do not address the more fundamental problem of getting a correct prior in a SAR setting like GO.

### Gaps
- No source found directly states a precise minimum sample-size threshold (e.g. "N positives needed for stable estimation") for any of these four methods in general; the closest concrete data point is Elkan & Noto's anecdotal ~20-example validation set achieving a close estimate in one experiment, which shouldn't be read as a general minimum-sample guarantee.
- No published, controlled study was found comparing EN vs. KM2 vs. TIcE vs. nnPU specifically on protein-function-prediction data (as opposed to UCI benchmark datasets or generic ML testbeds) — comparative performance figures cited above (e.g. computational speed) come from general ML papers, not domain-specific ones.

## What other techniques (beyond PU-learning) does the field use to handle incomplete GO annotations?

### Takeaway
The dominant, most widely-adopted approach is exploiting the ontology's true-path rule to propagate asserted annotations to ancestors (a label-enrichment technique, not a PU-correction technique); beyond that, the field mostly copes with incompleteness via evaluation-methodology design (CAFA's time-delayed no-knowledge/limited-knowledge benchmarks, curated negative-annotation benchmarks) rather than via label-noise-correcting training techniques, and via evidence-code-based filtering/weighting of which annotations are trusted as ground truth at all.

### Cited Findings
- **True-path-rule propagation**: GO relationships "is_a" and "part_of" are transitive, so "any protein annotated to a specific term is also implicitly annotated to all of its parents" — meaning every protein's small set of directly-asserted annotations implies a much larger set of true ancestor annotations that are rarely spelled out as explicit rows in raw annotation files. Non-transitive relations ("regulates," "positively/negatively regulates") must be excluded from this propagation because they break the true-path guarantee — illustrated with a concrete example (a "peptidase inhibitor activity" annotation does not imply "proteolysis," because the link passes through a "negatively regulates" edge) — [Gaudet & Dessimoz, GO Handbook](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.01875)
- **Curated negative-annotation benchmarking**: because the GO database "rarely stores which proteins do not possess a function" and an absent annotation could mean either a true negative or an as-yet-undiscovered positive, researchers built dedicated resources of expert-curated negative annotations — e.g. the **NoGO database**, which derives negative examples from expert-curated protein-family/phylogenetic-tree analysis rather than treating "unannotated" as "negative" — [Fernández & Friedberg (via search-aggregated summary), "Negative Example Selection for Protein Function Prediction: The NoGO Database," PLOS Comput Biol](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pcbi.1003644)
- A follow-on benchmark explicitly built to address CAFA's OWA blind spot — **"Benchmarking gene ontology function predictions using negative annotations"** — used a balanced set of positive and phylogenetic-tree-curated negative annotations, on the stated grounds that "the definition of a false positive in CAFA ... has not fully accounted for the open world assumption ... leading to a systematic underestimation of precision"; the same paper notes positive annotations are more informative farther from the ontology root while negative annotations are more informative closer to the root, an asymmetry curated negatives can exploit that raw absence-of-annotation cannot — [Zhou, Škunca, Dessimoz et al., Bioinformatics/ISMB 2020](https://academic.oup.com/bioinformatics/article/36/Supplement_1/i210/5870480)
- **CAFA's own time-delayed no-knowledge (NK) / limited-knowledge (LK) benchmark design** is itself a coping mechanism for OWA at the evaluation level rather than a label-correction technique: a protein qualifies as a "no-knowledge" benchmark if it had zero experimental GO annotations at time t₀ but accumulated at least one experimentally-evidenced term by time t₁; "limited-knowledge" proteins had experimental annotations in one or two of the three GO aspects (not all three) at t₀. This narrows evaluation to genuinely *new* ground truth accrued in a defined window, rather than asserting the full absence of annotation at t₀ was a true negative — [search-aggregated summary of CAFA evaluation methodology, referencing the CAFA challenge series papers](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13059-019-1835-8)
- CAFA training data itself is filtered by **evidence code**: participants are given "a curated training dataset consisting of all 142,246 proteins with GO annotations validated by experimental evidence codes" — i.e. automated/electronic (IEA-type) annotations, which are the most numerous but least individually trustworthy, are excluded from what counts as ground-truth positive signal for training — [CAFA challenge methodology, search-aggregated summary referencing the Genome Biology CAFA papers](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13059-016-1037-6)
- The GO handbook separately documents that even among evidence codes retained, quality is uneven: direct-assay evidence (IDA) is most reliable, while mutant-phenotype (IMP), genetic-interaction (IGI), physical-interaction (IPI), and expression-pattern (IEP) evidence are progressively weaker/more "guilt-by-association"-like, and roughly 25% of GO annotations may derive from high-throughput experiments that are biased toward a narrow set of high-level, low-information-content terms — [Gaudet & Dessimoz, GO Handbook](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.01875)
- The GO handbook explicitly recommends two practical remedies for the incompleteness problem in aggregate analyses: (1) "gauge the effect of incomplete annotations on conclusions by thinning annotations," and (2) "analysing successive, increasingly complete database releases" to check whether conclusions are stable as more annotations accrue — [Gaudet & Dessimoz, GO Handbook](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1602.01875)

### Inferences
- The field's center of gravity for handling OWA is clearly on the *evaluation/benchmark-design* side (NK/LK splits, curated-negative benchmarks, evidence-code filtering) rather than on the *training-time label-correction* side (PU-learning, self-training) — consistent with the earlier finding that PU-learning-specific methods for GO prediction are sparse. True-path propagation is the one training-time technique that is both near-universal and directly targets incompleteness, but it only recovers annotations that are logically implied by what's already asserted — it cannot recover a genuinely novel, unrelated true annotation the way a well-calibrated PU method in principle could.
- No literature-based/co-annotation confidence-scoring technique (e.g. scoring an unannotated pair's plausibility from co-annotation patterns of similar proteins) was surfaced as a named, established field-standard method distinct from ordinary sequence/network-based function prediction itself — it's plausible this is treated as indistinguishable from "protein function prediction" generally rather than as a specific "OWA-handling technique" in the literature.

### Gaps
- No dedicated paper on "self-training for GO annotation completion" (i.e., iteratively adding high-confidence model predictions back into the training-label set) was identified in this search pass; the review-referenced "label propagation" (random walk with restart) methods are the closest analog found, but they were described only as a PU-learning strategy for identifying reliable negatives, not as a general self-training-for-annotation-completion technique in the CAFA literature specifically.
- Could not confirm whether newer (post-2022) CAFA rounds (CAFA4/CAFA5, the latter concluding around 2024–2025) changed their NK/LK benchmark definitions or evidence-code policy in response to the OWA critiques cited above; a fetch of the "Advances in Protein Function Prediction from the Fifth CAFA Challenge" preprint was attempted but failed (HTTP 429 rate limit) and was not retried within this research pass.

## What do published critiques say about the practical difficulty of applying formal PU-learning theory here — e.g. class-prior estimation needing enough positives per label, against GO's long-tailed term-frequency distribution?

### Takeaway
No source found directly analyzes classical PU class-prior estimators' failure modes *specifically* against GO's long-tailed term-frequency distribution — this appears to be a genuine, largely unaddressed gap in the published literature, though the building blocks for the concern (well-documented small-sample instability of PU prior estimators; well-documented long-tailedness of GO term annotation counts) are each independently established.

### Cited Findings
- The 2022 PU-learning-in-bioinformatics review states generically that PU methods risk "overfitting ... when the unlabeled training set is overly small" and that PU classifiers are "sensitive" to imbalance between positive and unlabeled set sizes, recommending sub-sampling as a mitigation — a general (not GO-specific) statement of the small-sample failure mode — [Zhang et al. 2022](https://academic.oup.com/bib/article/23/1/bbab461/6415313)
- PU-GO's own reported limitation is that its GO-hierarchy-derived prior uses only raw per-term annotation frequency and a single shared hyperparameter π₀ across all terms — it does not report per-term calibration quality or discuss whether its prior estimate degrades for the rarest, most poorly-annotated GO terms specifically — [PMC11211813](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11211813/)
- TIcE's own validity condition (a lower-bound estimate of the label frequency c, derived from the purest achievable decision-tree leaf) is explicitly reported to require the SCAR assumption, which its own literature acknowledges is frequently violated in practice — but no source found ties this specifically to per-GO-term sample size or to GO's annotation-count distribution — [PULSNAR summary, arXiv 2303.08269](https://arxiv.org/html/2303.08269)

### Inferences
- Connecting cited facts from elsewhere in this research: GO's term-frequency distribution is documented elsewhere in this project's own work (see this repo's `generate_dataset.py`/`CLAUDE.md`, which imposes a `COARSE_MIN_ANNOTATIONS` = 50-protein floor specifically because terms below that threshold are treated as too sparse to model reliably) as extremely long-tailed, with many GO terms annotated to only a handful of proteins. Combined with the independently-documented fact that classical class-prior estimators (Elkan-Noto, KM2, TIcE) all derive their estimate from the pool of *labeled positive* examples for a given class, and that pool shrinks in direct proportion to a term's annotation count, it follows by direct inference (not by a cited source making this exact claim) that these estimators would be expected to produce high-variance, unreliable class-prior estimates for the great majority of GO terms — which are precisely the long-tail, sparsely-annotated ones. This is a reasoned inference bridging two independently-cited facts, not something any single source states outright.
- The absence of any source explicitly discussing this intersection (long-tail term frequency × PU class-prior reliability) reinforces the earlier finding that PU-learning-for-GO is an underexplored area: even the one purpose-built method found (PU-GO) sidesteps rigorous per-term class-prior estimation by using a simple frequency-ratio heuristic rather than a formal estimator like KM2 or TIcE, which is itself indirect evidence that the authors may have judged formal class-prior estimation impractical at the scale/sparsity of real GO term distributions (though PU-GO's own paper does not state this reasoning explicitly, so this remains an inference).

### Gaps
- No published critique, commentary, or negative result was found that explicitly states "classical PU class-prior estimation (Elkan-Noto/KM2/TIcE) fails or is impractical for GO/CAFA-style protein function prediction because of the long-tailed term-frequency distribution." If such a critique exists in the literature, it was not surfaced by the search queries used in this research pass, and this should be treated as an open/underexplored question rather than a settled negative finding.
- No source was found giving an explicit numeric threshold (e.g. "estimators need >N labeled positives to be reliable") that could be directly compared against GO's known per-term annotation-count distribution to make the inference above a cited fact rather than a reasoned inference.
